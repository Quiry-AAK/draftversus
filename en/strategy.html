Draft & Tactics Strategy Guide

In DraftVersus what wins matches is not the squad with the highest ratings, but the squad built to the right balance. This guide collects the principles that actually work — for the draft order, the duel and your tactical settings. If you don't know the basic flow yet, start with How to Play.

1 · The draft: where to start

Each round of the draft opens a position and you choose from the candidates. Because the pool is limited, the early rounds decide your squad's skeleton.

  • Build the spine first. Goalkeeper, centre-back, central midfield and centre-forward are the backbone. Weakness there shows up in every match; a thin wing can be papered over with tactics.
  • Hurry for scarce positions. Good goalkeepers and genuine centre-backs are limited in the pool. Midfielders usually stay plentiful to the end; goalkeepers do not.
  • Watch your rival's needs. Which positions have they filled, which are still empty? Taking a position they are forced to fill weakens what's left for them.
  • Avoid extremes. Dropping three positions to the 70s for one 90-rated star costs you across the series. The match engine rewards collective strength.

2 · Position suitability: the silent loss

The game colours the positions a footballer can play: green best, yellow workable, red struggling. Take that warning seriously.

Rule of thumb: an 85-rated player out of position is usually worse than a 78-rated player in his best role. The loss isn't only numerical — a misplaced player also positions himself poorly and reads his duties badly.

Flexible footballers (those who cover several positions) are valuable for exactly this reason: they let you patch holes as the draft goes on.

3 · Age and potential: choose by series length

Every footballer has an OVR (today's strength) and a POT (potential ceiling). The gap between them shows how much they can improve during the series.

  • Bo3 (short series): current strength wins. An 18-year-old rated 74/97 cannot reach his ceiling before the series ends.
  • Bo5 (long series): young, high-potential players improve match by match and can decide the closing games.
  • Beware older stars: highly rated veterans can decline as the series progresses and are more vulnerable to fatigue.

4 · The steal/protect duel: a guessing game

Each round you make two simultaneous decisions: who you want to steal from your rival, and who you want to protect. Both stay hidden until you lock in. A few useful instincts:

  • Protecting your highest OVR isn't always right. Your rival expects that and won't bother trying. What really needs protection is the player you cannot replace — your only real goalkeeper, your only centre-back.
  • Target their weak spot when stealing. Taking a player from a position where they have no cover lands two blows at once: you gain, and that position collapses.
  • Count what you give back. A successful steal sends one of your own players to them. If the net gain is negative, the steal is pointless.
  • Don't be predictable. If you protect your best player every round, your rival reads it and strikes elsewhere.

5 · Matching formation and philosophy

There are five formations (4-4-2, 4-3-3, 4-2-3-1, 3-5-2, 5-3-2) and six philosophies (Possession, Counter, High Press, Defensive Block, Direct, Wing Play). What matters is that the two — and your squad — fit each other.

  • Possession — needs midfielders with passing and dribbling. Works with 4-3-3 and 4-2-3-1. With a weak midfield you simply lose the ball in dangerous areas.
  • Counter — needs quick wingers and forwards. Very effective against a rival who wants the ball. Choosing it with a slow attack is wasted effort.
  • High Press — needs physique and stamina, and burns fatigue faster. With a thin or ageing squad you collapse late in matches.
  • Defensive Block — needs strong centre-backs and a disciplined midfield. When the strength gap is against you, this is the safest way to stay in the game.
  • Direct — needs a physically strong striker; it wastes a technical midfield.
  • Wing Play — needs attacking full-backs and genuine wingers; it contradicts narrow shapes like 3-5-2.

6 · Mentality: your risk dial

Mentality has five steps: Very Cautious · Cautious · Balanced · Bold · Very Bold. Adjusting it to the flow of the match beats fixing it at kick-off.

  • Ahead and late in the game → pull back and protect the score.
  • Behind with time running out → push forward; goals don't come without risk.
  • Rival clearly stronger → start cautious and wait for a counter.
  • "Very Bold" means staying exposed; use it only when you must score.

7 · Team focus: where do the 9 points go?

You spread nine points across six areas (Pressing, Tempo, Width, Defensive Line, Creativity, Physicality). Spreading them evenly is the worst choice — it creates no advantage anywhere.

  • Specialise. Loading two or three areas beats sprinkling across six.
  • Stay consistent with your squad. Loading Pressing with a physically weak squad brings fatigue forward.
  • The Defensive Line is a trade-off: a high line increases pressure but opens space behind you. If the rival has a quick striker, drop it.
  • Creativity helps create chances in stuck matches; it is wasted when you're protecting a lead.

8 · Roles and duties

Every position has its own roles (a goalkeeper can be a Sweeper Keeper or a Line Keeper; full-backs can be Attacking · Standard · Defensive). Each player also gets one of Attack · Balanced · Defend.

The key point: putting the whole team on attack doesn't make you attacking, it makes you unbalanced. A healthy pattern is usually this — defenders defending, one midfielder balanced, wingers and forwards attacking. If you play a high defensive line, keeping at least one full-back on defend protects the space behind.

9 · In-match management

  • Watch fatigue. Falling stamina directly reduces pace and tackling accuracy. Don't save your substitutions for the end; the 60th–70th minute is usually right.
  • Take injured players off immediately. Leaving them on breaks both that position and your balance.
  • Mind booked players. Aggressive duties raise the risk of a second card; soften their role or replace them.
  • Use half-time. Changing shape and focus based on what you saw in the first half is the cheapest way to turn a match around.
  • Read the pressure meter. If you're under constant pressure, lowering mentality and dropping the defensive line usually stops the bleeding.

10 · Extra principles for online matches

  • Your tactics are hidden. Your rival cannot see your shape until kick-off — use that and pick something unexpected.
  • Remember the rival is human. Unlike the AI, a human opponent bluffs and reads your habits.
  • Change across the series. What worked in match one is recognised in match two; even small adjustments unsettle them.

These are starting points rather than fixed rules — the real learning happens on the pitch. Start a series now and find your own pattern.
